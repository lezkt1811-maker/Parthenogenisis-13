CHAPTER VII

THE STOLEN NAME

Before the Daughter learned the names
Of all the stars above,
She learned that names could carry fear,
And names could carry love.

She learned that names could open doors
And welcome travelers in,
But names could also build a wall
And mark who could not enter in.

She learned a name could hold a memory,
A promise, or a prayer,
And that a name could disappear
While its echo lingered there.

One morning, as the mist arose
From valleys far below,
The Daughter heard a distant cry
From somewhere she did not know.

It was not the cry of someone wounded.
It was not the cry of pain.
It was the sound of someone searching
For a word that would not come again.

She followed it beyond the river,
Past the stones that marked the road,
Until she reached a ruined temple
Where the oldest stories glowed.

Upon its walls were carved the names
Of queens, of kings, of those who'd led.
Some were crowned with golden symbols.
Some were honored among the dead.

But other names had been chiseled out,
Their letters scraped away.
The empty spaces told a story
That the remaining words would not say.

The Daughter touched the stone
And felt its ancient, silent grief.

"Who were these?" she asked the Serpent.
"Who has taken their relief?"

The Serpent coiled beside the wall.

"These are names that once were spoken.
These are lives that once were known.
These are voices that were carried
Far beyond the flesh and bone.

"But time is not the only keeper
Of the stories that remain.
Sometimes hands have changed the record.
Sometimes power has changed the name."

The Daughter traced a hollow letter
With the tip of her own hand.

"Then tell me who has done this.
Tell me who gave this command."

The Serpent answered carefully:

"I can tell you what is here.
I can show you what is missing.
I can name the shape of fear.

"But a space upon a wall
Cannot tell us every hand.
We must seek the surviving records
Before we say we understand.

"For an absence is a question,
Not a verdict on its own.
And the truth deserves a careful search
Before a claim is carved in stone."

The Daughter lowered her fingers.

She had learned that longing
Could make a person see a doorway
Where there was only stone.

She would not turn an empty space
Into a certainty alone.

So she searched among the fragments,
Through the dust and broken clay.
She gathered what remained there
Of the words that lived before her day.

She found the name of one who healed,
A woman known for skill,
Whose remedies had crossed the hills
And calmed the fevered ill.

Her name had once been written
Beside the names of learned men.
But someone had removed her title,
Then written another in again.

She found a woman who had taught
The children how to read the sky,
Who knew the wandering planets
And could tell when stars would rise.

Her name survived in one old ledger,
But nowhere in the royal song.
The record called her knowledge useful,
Then failed to say who had made it strong.

She found a mother who had led
Her people through a famine year,
Whose choices kept the village living,
Whose wisdom had outlasted fear.

Her name remained upon a tablet,
But the story gave the credit
To a ruler who had arrived afterward
And claimed the victory as his.

The Daughter sat among the fragments
As the afternoon grew dim.

"How many stories have been altered?
How many songs have lost their hymn?
How many women lived and labored
While another took the praise?
How many names became a whisper
Through the turning of the days?"

The Serpent did not answer
With a number it could not know.

"More than we can count," it said,
"Perhaps. But let the evidence show
What each surviving record tells us.
Let the missing pieces speak
As questions that deserve our care,
Not as answers we must seek.

"We honor those whose names survive.
We honor those erased.
But we must not replace the truth
With another invented face."

The Daughter closed her eyes.

She saw a thousand nameless women,
Not as shadows without lives,
But as people who had dreamed,
Who had chosen, who had strived.

They had laughed beneath the moonlight.
They had argued, sung, and wept.
They had built and taught and tended.
They had made the promises they kept.

They had known the taste of berries,
The warmth of sunlight on the skin.
They had watched their children sleeping
And wondered what might have been.

They had been more than symbols.
They had been more than someone's wife.
They had been more than vessels
For the continuation of a life.

They had been whole and breathing people,
With their own desires and fears.
And the Daughter felt the weight of them
Across a thousand years.

"Mother," she whispered to the wind,
"How can I restore a name
When the hands that knew its letters
Have been carried from the frame?"

The wind moved through the temple.
The dust rose from the floor.
And the Mother came in silence
Through a half-remembered door.

"You cannot call every name back,"
The Mother gently said.
"You cannot know each hidden thought
Of those who now are dead.

"But you can learn what has survived.
You can preserve what still is true.
You can say, 'This part is certain.
This part is not yet known to you.'

"You can honor what was taken
Without claiming what you lack.
You can leave a space for questions
And refuse to fill each crack.

"You can listen to the living
Who remember what was done.
You can seek the scattered records
And compare them one by one.

"You can teach the next generation
That a story is not whole
When it leaves the names of people
Who gave their labor, heart, and soul."

The Daughter looked upon the Mother.

"But what if they have changed the words?
What if all that now remains
Is a story told by victors
Who have dressed their power as chains?"

The Mother answered:

"Then you must learn to read with care.
Ask who spoke and who was heard.
Ask who kept the oldest record.
Ask what evidence supports each word.

"Ask what other witnesses tell you.
Ask what time has changed or spared.
Ask which voices were excluded
And whether anyone compared
The surviving pieces carefully
Before declaring what was there.

"Do not fear a complicated answer.
Do not fear a truth that bends
The story you once hoped to find.
A truth that can withstand your questions
Is a stronger, truer friend."

The Daughter took a piece of parchment
And began to write.

Not the names she wished were present,
Not a history made of light,
But the names that still survived her,
The records she could trace,
The questions left unanswered,
And the evidence in place.

She wrote:

Here lived a woman who healed.

Here taught a woman who watched the stars.

Here led a woman whose name survives
Though others claimed her part.

Here lies a space where words once stood.
We do not know who took them out.

We will preserve the space as found
And keep alive the honest doubt.

She wrote of those remembered.
She wrote of those unknown.
She would not call an empty page
A truth already known.

And as she wrote, the Serpent watched
The ink move through the light.

For the Daughter had discovered
That remembering required more
Than anger at the night.

It required the patience to investigate,
The courage to say "I don't know,"
And the willingness to change a story
When new evidence began to show.

The Mother touched the parchment.

"Now you understand the name.

"A name is not a body.
A record is not a life.
A title cannot tell you
All the courage, joy, and strife.

"But when a name is carried honestly,
It can help a life endure.
And when a record is corrected,
It can make a history more sure.

"To restore a name is not to own it.
To remember is not to possess.
The one who tells the story
Must be willing to confess
What is known and what is missing,
What is certain, what is guessed.

"For the truth is not a garment
You can tailor to your need.
It is a living path of questions,
And it asks that you proceed
With an open hand, a steady heart,
And a mind that can be freed."

The Daughter rose at evening
And walked beyond the wall.

She could not restore each letter.
She could not recover all.

But she carried what remained there
With a tenderness made strong.
She would not let the lost be forgotten,
Nor pretend she knew their song.

She would ask the living questions.
She would search the ancient page.
She would honor women's labor
Across each place and age.

And when she found a story
That had left a voice unheard,
She would bring that voice to daylight
Without inventing every word.

The Serpent followed softly,
Its scales reflecting stars.

"Remember," it said, "the past is not
A kingdom that is ours.

"We are guests among its fragments.
We are keepers of its flame.
We may carry what survives us.
We may question every name.

"But we must leave the door unbarred
For what we do not know.
The past is not our possession.
It is a field where truth can grow."

That night the Daughter dreamed
Of names upon the ocean floor.
Some were written in the sand.
Some were carved beside a door.

Some were spoken by a grandmother
To a child upon her knee.
Some survived in songs and letters.
Some were lost to history.

But none were truly made meaningless
By the silence of the years.
For every life had once been lived,
With its own hopes and fears.

And the Daughter understood at last
What the Mother meant to say:

A stolen name can wound a people,
But honest remembrance lights a way.

Do not fill the gaps with wishes.
Do not let the powerful define
Every voice that came before you.
Seek the evidence. Preserve the line.

And far beneath the waters,
Where the oldest memories lie,
The Mother kept the first remembrance
Safe beneath the turning sky.

For before the first father spoke his name,
Before the first kingdom claimed the flame,
There were lives that mattered without permission.

And the world had not yet learned
How much it could lose
By forgetting their names.
