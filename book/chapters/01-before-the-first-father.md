CHAPTER I

BEFORE THE FIRST FATHER

In the beginning, there was no beginning.

There was no dawn to announce it.
No sun to witness it.
No voice to proclaim that something had begun.

There was only the Vastness.

And within the Vastness, the Mother.

She had no name, for names had not yet been invented.

She had no body as the living would one day understand a body. She had no skin to separate her from the darkness, no eyes to distinguish the distant from the near.

She was not standing upon the earth.

There was no earth.

She was not floating among the stars.

There were no stars.

She was the possibility from which distance would one day emerge, the stillness from which movement would awaken, the unseen fullness that existed before anyone had thought to call something empty.

She was not waiting for a creator.

She was the possibility of creation itself.

And yet, for all that she contained, one mystery remained.

She did not know herself.

Not because she lacked wisdom, but because there was nothing outside her against which wisdom could be measured. There was no reflection in which to see her face, no voice to return her words, no other presence to say, I hear you.

She was everything.

And because she was everything, she had never encountered anything other.

So she listened.

She listened to the silence that had no edges.

She listened to the darkness that had no depth.

She listened until listening itself became the first movement.

And in that movement, something stirred.

Not a man.

Not a woman.

Not a child.

A question.

What might I become?

The question traveled through her like the first tremor beneath an ancient sea.

For the first time, the Vastness was not merely what existed.

It was what might exist.

The Mother felt the difference.

Possibility had become desire.

Desire had become intention.

And intention, though it had no shape, was already changing the silence.

She placed her hands upon the place where her heart would one day be.

And she spoke:

"Let there be a becoming.

Let what is possible discover its shape.

Let what is hidden find a way to appear.

Let there be something
that may look upon me
and know that I am here."

She expected no answer.

There had never been an answer.

But the darkness trembled.

And from the place where her intention touched the Vastness, a small light appeared.

It was no larger than a thought.

It held no sun within it, no moon, no world, no living thing.

Yet it was distinct from her.

The Mother drew back.

Not in fear.

In wonder.

For she had encountered the first mystery of creation:

Something could emerge from her without being all that she was.

She approached the light.

"Who are you?" she asked.

The light did not answer.

It had no language.

"What shall I call you?"

The light did not answer.

It had no name.

"Do you belong to me?"

The light brightened.

And the Mother understood that belonging was not the same as ownership.

The light had emerged through her intention, but it was not a possession to be locked away. It was a new possibility, a beginning that would one day hold beginnings of its own.

She had not become smaller by creating it.

She had become more.

And so the Mother laughed.

It was not the laughter of a queen who had conquered a kingdom.

It was the laughter of a being who had discovered that the mystery was larger than she had imagined.

Her laughter moved through the Vastness.

Where it passed, the darkness began to ripple.

The ripples became currents.

The currents became waters.

The waters folded into themselves, and within their depths, the first patterns of life began to dream.

The Mother watched them form.

She did not command every shape.

She did not name every movement.

She allowed the possibilities to unfold.

For she was learning that creation was not merely the act of making.

It was also the courage to let what had been made become something new.

And somewhere in the first waters, something long and luminous opened its eyes.

A serpent.

It watched the Mother without fear.

It watched the light without envy.

And when the first skin of its becoming grew too small, it shed that skin and moved forward into the waters.

The Mother recognized the lesson.

To become was to change.

To change was not necessarily to betray what had come before.

And to outgrow an old form was not proof that the old form had never mattered.

She reached toward the serpent.

It curled around her wrist, not as a chain, but as a living circle.

The Mother looked at the light.

She looked at the waters.

She looked at the serpent, who carried the memory of what it had been without allowing that memory to imprison what it might become.

Then she spoke the first teaching of the new universe.

"Let no beginning mistake itself for the only beginning.

Let no form believe itself the final form.

Let no name become a prison for the thing it names.

For life is not a statue.

Life is a becoming.

And what becomes
must be permitted
to become again."

The waters deepened.

The light multiplied.

And the Vastness, which had once held only possibility, now held a thousand possibilities waiting to awaken.

The Mother stood among them.

Not above them.

Not apart from them.

She was their beginning, but she was also learning from what they became.

And in the deep places where no light had yet reached, a new question began to form.

If the Mother could bring forth something other than herself, could that new creation also create?

Could the Daughter, when she came, become a beginning in her own right?

Could the life that emerged from life possess a power that did not require the permission of its origin?

The Mother did not yet know.

She would have to discover the answer.

And so the universe continued becoming.

The first light traveled outward.

The waters gathered their tides.

The serpent carried its memory into the deep.

And the Mother, who had existed before the first beginning, watched the world unfold without surrendering the mystery that had made it possible.

She had created something new.

But she had not created the last thing.

For the first law of the Undivided Mother was this:

That which gives life does not lose its own.

And that which is born
is not born merely
to remain as it was.

Thus the first chapter came to its close.

Not with an ending.

With a question.

And questions, the Mother would learn, were among the oldest forms of creation.
